# Customer discovery interview guide

Use this guide to prepare or assess interviews. Adapt language to the interviewee's role and context. Treat any proposed duration or sample size as a planning choice.

## Before the conversation

- Write the decision the interview should inform and the assumptions it could contradict.
- Select participants using concrete eligibility criteria. Recruit users and buyers separately when their knowledge differs.
- Include people outside the founder's immediate network where feasible.
- Request only information needed for the research. Agree how notes or recordings may be used before recording.
- Prepare a place for source date, participant pseudonym, organization pseudonym, role, recruitment channel, interviewer, and exact source locator.

## Suggested conversation structure

| Part | Purpose | Example prompts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Context | Understand role and relevant responsibilities | "What are you responsible for when this work happens?" |
| Recent event | Reconstruct a real instance | "Walk me through the last time you needed to do this." |
| Workflow | Understand actual steps and alternatives | "What did you do first? Which tools or people were involved?" |
| Consequences | Establish importance and tradeoffs | "What happened when it took longer or went wrong?" |
| Attempts | Learn about effort already invested | "What have you tried to improve it? What happened?" |
| Buying process | Identify payer, budget, and constraints | "How was the current solution selected and paid for?" |
| Next step | Seek a relevant, voluntary commitment | "Would a follow-up session to observe that workflow be useful?" |

Use open follow-ups: "What happened next?", "Can you give an example?", and "How do you know?" Ask one question at a time. Avoid presenting the solution before the interviewee has described the problem.

## Role-specific follow-ups

### B2B

- Ask who performs the work, who notices the consequence, who sponsors change, and who approves spending.
- Ask about actual budget ownership, procurement, integration needs, switching costs, and decision timing.
- Record when a user is speculating about a buyer's preferences. Propose speaking with the buyer rather than treating speculation as verified purchasing evidence.
- Count multiple people in one organization separately from multiple independent buying accounts.

### B2C

- Ask who uses the service and who pays; identify any family, institution, or employer acting as payer.
- Explore the occasion, frequency, alternatives, perceived risk, and actual purchase behavior.
- Distinguish the user's motivation from the payer's decision criteria.
- Avoid assuming that occasional or seasonal needs imply monthly recurring use.

## Neutral alternatives to leading questions

| Avoid as a discovery question | Ask instead |
| --- | --- |
| "Would an AI tool save you time?" | "Where did you spend time in the last instance?" |
| "Would you pay EUR 30 for this?" | "What do you use today, what does it cost, and how was that purchase decided?" |
| "Would you use these five features?" | "Which step caused the greatest difficulty, and what did you do?" |
| "Our approach is easier, right?" | "Show me how you would complete this task with the prototype." |

A concrete price discussion or purchase test can follow problem discovery. Record that it is an offer test, its exact scope, and whether the response was hypothetical or an actual commitment.

## Note structure

For each material observation, record:

- Participant and organization pseudonyms.
- Role and relevance to the target segment.
- Event date or stated recency; use "not provided" if unknown.
- Reported behavior or observed action.
- Exact quotation with locator, or explicitly labeled paraphrase.
- Numbers with unit, period, and whether measured or estimated.
- Current alternative and consequence.
- Interviewer's interpretation in a separate field.
- Contradictory evidence or uncertainty.
- Agreed next step, if any, using its actual status.

## Synthesis discipline

Review complete available notes before extracting a theme. Preserve counterexamples. Deduplicate repeated accounts of the same event. Treat a silence in the notes as missing information, not proof that a problem was absent. Report what the sample can support and what still needs investigation.
